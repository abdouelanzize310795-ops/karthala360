Mount Karthala, volcano and forest

The highest peak in the Comoros is one of the most active volcanoes in the Indian Ocean. Its slopes carry a forest where birds live that are found nowhere else on Earth.

Key facts

The volcano in figures

2,361 m

The highest point in the Comoros archipelago. The volcano covers the whole southern half of Grande Comore (Ngazidja).

30 × 15 km

A shield volcano with long, regular slopes built by basalt lava flows, extended by two fissure zones: 25 km to the north and 12 km to the south-east.

3 × 4 km

The summit caldera, formed by successive collapses. In its centre lies the Choungou Chahalé crater, about 300 m deep.

20+ eruptions

Since the early 19th century. The last confirmed eruption was in January 2007; a yellow alert was raised in 2022. The Karthala Volcano Observatory has monitored it since 1988.

26,214 ha

The area of Karthala National Park. The massif has also been a Ramsar site since 2006 and is an Important Bird Area.

Landscapes

From the shore to the caldera, five layers of nature

Climbing from Mvouni, you pass through fields and villages, strawberry guava thickets, the dense rainforest, then a shrubby savannah of giant heather, and finally the low scrub and bare rock of the summit.

Grande Comore has almost no permanent rivers: rain seeps into the porous lava and feeds underground water tables. The Karthala forest, which captures water from the clouds, plays a vital role for the island’s water.

The five layers in detail

On site

What you see at the top

At the edge of the caldera, the landscape changes at once: after the forest and the heather, a vast plateau of lava and ash, enclosed by walls, with the Choungou Chahalé in the centre, a crater about 1.3 km by 800 m. A lake filled its floor from 1991 to 2005, before disappearing during the April 2005 eruption.

On a clear day, the view reaches the coast. But the summit is often in the clouds: that is one of the reasons trips start early, and the view is never guaranteed.
